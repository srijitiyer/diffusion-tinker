\vspace{-1.5em}

\noindent\textbf{diffusion-tinker} runs RL post-training for diffusion models: give it a model, a reward, and prompts, and it runs one of six algorithms (FlowGRPO, DDRL, DRaFT, DiffusionDPO, DDPO, SFT) behind one \code{Trainer}/\code{Config} API on top of \code{diffusers}. Targets SD3.5 and FLUX.1.

This version supersedes the earlier report. An audit found ten defects, two of which invalidate most of what the previous version claimed. The headline one: the SDE sampler was singular at its first timestep, which destroyed every rollout at the noise level we ran OCR at. Details in \code{AUDIT.md}; the consequences are in \S\ref{sec:retract}.

\section{The bug that explains the rest}

\code{sde\_step\_with\_logprob} uses $\sigma_t = \text{noise}\cdot\sqrt{\sigma/(1-\sigma)}$ as the diffusion coefficient. The SD3.5 and FLUX schedules start at $\sigma$ exactly $1.0$, so that term diverges. A clamp kept it finite but left the first Euler step multiplying the latent by $-55.8$ at noise $0.7$ (and $-0.16$ at $0.1$, so the two noise levels were not even comparable). Every later step sits between $0.73$ and $1.00$.

The algebra is right. Our mean is $x + dt\,[v + \tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2\sigma}(x + (1-\sigma)v)]$, the reverse SDE for rectified flow with score $\nabla\log p_t = -(x+(1-\sigma)v)/\sigma$, and it matches the reference. The score is finite at $\sigma=1$; only the diffusion coefficient blows up. Capping it so the step stays contractive fixes it and, on the real schedules, changes nothing after step~0.

At noise $0.7$ the rollout reward was \emph{exactly} zero with \emph{exactly} zero within-group spread. The GRPO advantage is $(r-\bar r)/(\text{std}+\epsilon)$, so zero spread means zero advantage and the epoch was skipped outright. Eval never showed this because eval samples at noise $0$, where the term vanishes: a noise-0 eval of the same prompts scored $0.569$ while the rollout scored $0.005$.

This also settles the importance ratio. It sat near $0.92$ for weeks and was blamed on bf16 rounding, gradient checkpointing, and CFG in turn. It was this, and CFG was never involved (measured $0.92$ on versus $0.94$ off, and sampling stores the encoded negative embeddings that replay reuses, so both branches see identical conditioning).

\section{Eval was measuring noise}

\code{\_evaluate} used \code{train\_prompts[:4]} and passed no generator. So it was four prompts, taken from the training set, sampled from whatever global RNG state training happened to leave behind, meaning different latents every epoch. That is the $\pm 0.5$ epoch-to-epoch swing in every curve this project has produced. Those curves were not noisy measurements of a trend, they were noise.

Eval now takes a configurable held-out prompt list, repeats each prompt, draws from a dedicated seeded generator so every epoch sees identical latents, chunks sampling so a large set does not OOM the VAE decode, reports a standard error, and runs at epoch 0 so there is a baseline to compare against.

\label{sec:results}

All four are above the untrained baseline after two epochs, by roughly two to three standard errors, and the ratio stays within $0.5\%$ of 1 throughout. FlowGRPO has a second point: $0.494 \pm 0.066$ at epoch 2 and $0.702 \pm 0.056$ at epoch 4, against a baseline of $0.300 \pm 0.064$. That last gap is $0.402$ against a combined standard error of $0.085$, so about $4.7\sigma$, so the reward is unambiguously climbing, which is the thing that never happened before the sampler was fixed.

The last row uses \code{use\_monotonic\_transform}, which is off by default and should stay off: the DDRL authors do not use it, because of the negative exponential. It is included only to show that the re-centring fix works, and it is not a configuration to quote results from. The canonical DDRL is an anchor with the transform off, and those runs are in progress.

The reason to avoid it is exactly the one that made it collapse here. $\lambda(A) = -e^{-A}$ has range $(-\infty, 0)$, so applied to a zero-mean advantage every value is negative and the term $-A\cdot\text{ratio}$ discourages every sampled action, with the unbounded side making it violent. Re-centring per group restores a valid policy-gradient weight, which is why the run no longer collapses, but that repairs an option rather than establishing a method.

It climbs and then holds: $0.893$, $0.892$, $0.873$, $0.851$ at epochs 4 through 10. The previous report's collapse appeared around epoch 10, so passing that point at $0.851$, still $0.55$ above baseline, settles the question. The mild drift from the peak is within about one standard error per step and is not the failure mode described before, which crashed the reward outright. That is the shape the anchor is supposed to produce: reach a high level and stay there. The run continues to epoch 10, where the old collapse appeared, before non-collapse is stated as a result.

\section{FlowGRPO($\beta{=}0$) and DDRL($\beta{=}0$) are the same thing}

They are identical in source, not merely similar. The base advantage clamps the group-normalised reward to $\pm 5$, and DDRL's reduces to exactly that once \code{beta\_temp}$=1$ skips the divide and the monotonic transform is off. FlowGRPO's only extra term, \code{use\_grpo\_guard}, defaults off, and at $\beta=0$ both assemble the same clipped-PPO loss.

Empirically, at epoch 0 from the same seed, both selected the same prompt subset in the same order and produced identical per-prompt rewards
\[
[0.08,\; 0.08,\; 0.00,\; 0.29,\; 0.17,\; 0.17,\; 0.08,\; 0.17],
\]
identical mean reward $0.1302$, identical mean advantage $0$, and identical ratio $0.9977$. Only the printed loss differs in the fourth decimal ($0.0005$ versus $0.0004$), which is bf16 GEMM nondeterminism between two processes. Curves diverge after the first update because RL amplifies that difference through its own feedback loop, so per-epoch equality past epoch 0 is not achievable across processes and is not the right thing to ask for.

\section{DDPO was never DDPO}

\code{DDPOTrainer} never overrode \code{\_compute\_advantages}, so it inherited the within-batch group-normalised tracker and was FlowGRPO with a different clip range. The claim in its own docstring, that it lacks group normalisation, was false, and backwards historically: \code{PerPromptStatTracker} comes from DDPO, and the real one keeps a rolling per-prompt reward history with a fallback to batch statistics.

It now uses that. The signature is visible in the logs above: FlowGRPO and DDRL report mean advantage \emph{exactly} $0$, because group normalisation forces it, while DDPO reports $0.089$ then $0.101$, because it normalises against history instead. Two different estimators, and on this task DDPO climbs fastest. \textbf{Every earlier DDPO number should be read as a FlowGRPO number.}

\section{DDRL's monotonic transform}

$\lambda(A) = -e^{-A}$ is a legitimate monotonic, risk-averse reweighting, but its range is $(-\infty, 0)$, so applied to a zero-mean advantage every value comes out negative. In $-A\cdot\text{ratio}$ that discourages every sampled action, so the policy walks away from its own distribution, and the unbounded negative side makes it violent. That, not a weak anchor, is what the earlier report called ``DDRL collapses''.

The theorem does not have this problem because it maximises over $p_\theta$ as a distribution, so $\int p_\theta = 1$ is carried by a multiplier and the stationary solution is still normalised. The PPO surrogate enforces no such constraint. Subtracting a per-group baseline restores the zero-mean advantage the surrogate needs and leaves the policy gradient unbiased, since $\mathbb{E}[(A-b)\nabla\log\pi] = \mathbb{E}[A\nabla\log\pi]$, while $\lambda$'s monotonicity preserves the ranking exactly.

\section{What the previous report got wrong}
\label{sec:retract}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item ``DDRL collapses without a sufficient anchor; FlowGRPO's KL is more robust.'' That was the un-centred transform.
\item ``DDPO is genuinely weaker because it lacks a group-relative advantage.'' It had the identical estimator.
\item ``The ratio is $0.92$ because of bf16 / checkpointing / CFG.'' It was the $\sigma=1$ singularity.
\item ``Detection rewards need noise $0.1$, OCR needs $0.7$.'' An artifact of the same bug.
\item ``The FLUX path is validated.'' Only schnell. FLUX.1-dev could not build a schedule at all, and DDRL with an anchor and SFT were unimplemented for FLUX.
\item Any eval curve produced before this audit, including the grid figure, since eval had no fixed seed.
\end{itemize}

\section{FLUX.1-dev}

FLUX.1-dev runs end to end. Four epochs of FlowGRPO at 512px, LoRA rank 16 (26.1\,M trainable): reward $0.312 \to 0.806$, importance ratio $0.9992 \to 0.9998$, peak memory $36.5$\,GB, exit 0. So the 12\,B model plus a LoRA training step fits with roughly $11.5$\,GB of headroom.

DDRL with a data anchor also runs there, which matters because that is a different code path: the flow-matching loss needs packed latents, rotary position ids, a guidance scalar and $\text{timestep}=\sigma$ rather than SD3's $\sigma\times1000$. Measured over its first epochs: \code{data\_loss} steady near $0.42$, ratio $0.9993 \to 0.9999$, mean advantage exactly $0$.

The ratio is the load-bearing number in both. FLUX builds its schedule differently from SD3 (dynamic shifting, which is what the missing \code{mu} crashed on), so $0.999$ there says the $\sigma=1$ cap is a property of the SDE rather than a patch fitted to one schedule.

One result worth stating plainly: FLUX's untrained OCR is $0.792$ where SD3.5's is $0.300$. FLUX is already good at rendering text, so the task has almost no headroom left, and its held-out curve is flat ($0.792 \to 0.787$) while its training reward climbs $0.167 \to 0.800$. That is the headroom argument applying across models rather than across rewards, and it means OCR is the wrong task on which to demonstrate anything on FLUX even though it is the right one on SD3.5.

\section{Other trainers}

\textbf{DRaFT} had never been measured on held-out data. It overrides \code{train()} and so never called \code{\_evaluate}, meaning its long-standing $5.9 \to 9.6$ was the training reward on the prompts it directly optimises. With a held-out set it gives $5.440 \to 5.768$ across 13 evals. It also could not run at all until two memory defects were fixed: eval was leaving the text encoders on the GPU, undoing the offload DRaFT performs specifically to make room for the reward backward, and prompt pre-encoding pushed every prompt through T5 in one call. Peak memory went from OOM at $23.19$\,GB to $17.7$\,GB.

\textbf{SFT} converges and then overfits. Held-out flow-matching loss on 16 unseen examples at fixed noise and timesteps: $0.21184 \to 0.19742$ at its minimum, then back up to $0.20035$. The training loss falls the whole way, so the turnover is invisible without a held-out set, and it is an argument for the \code{early\_stop\_patience} the config already supports. That measurement came from a callback defined in a cluster run script, so it existed for that one run and for nobody calling \code{SFTTrainer} directly; it is now inside the trainer, and a fresh run reproduces the shape ($0.26583 \to 0.25219$, flat from step 150).

\textbf{DiffusionDPO} was recorded as blocked on Pick-a-Pic being gated. That was wrong: both versions 404 now, so accepting the terms would not have helped, and an ungated mirror carries the same schema. What actually blocked it was two defects, a missing $0.5$ in the loss that made the paper's $\beta=5000$ behave like $10000$, and a loader that assumed decoded images where the dataset stores raw jpeg bytes. Over 2000 steps it separates the pair, but one-sidedly: the loser's loss rises in 34/40 logged steps and the implicit reward is positive in 27/40, while the winner's loss falls in only 15/40, which is below chance.

It also had no held-out metric of any kind, only its own training loss --- the same failure as DRaFT. It now reports preference accuracy on unseen pairs, the fraction where the implicit reward ranks the winner higher. At step 0 that reads exactly $0.500$ with a mean margin of exactly $0.00000$, which is the initialisation check the trainer never had: LoRA's $B$ is zero, so the policy and the adapter-disabled reference must be the same network and every pair must tie. Over the run it peaks at $0.610$ and drifts back to $0.540$, mean $0.553$. That looks significant if you average ten evals and quote a standard error of $0.016$, and it is not: the 100 held-out pairs are fixed across evals by design, so the binomial error from having only 100 of them never averages down. The right error is $0.05$, putting the peak at about 2\,SE and the mean at 1. Two further defects surfaced in the same pass --- it drew independent noise for winner and loser where the reference shares one draw, and it still checkpointed the entire transformer.

\section{Anchor strength: what a sweep can and cannot show}

Haotian asked for $\beta = 0$, $0.01$ and upward. An earlier grid did sweep both regularizer weights, so the sweep existed --- but it passed no held-out prompts and no eval seed, so it scored the first four \emph{training} prompts, and it ran on counting 2--4 objects where the base model is at $0.792$ and every arm saturates at $1.000$. A sweep whose arms all reach the ceiling measures the ceiling.

The replacement runs on counting 6--9 objects, the one task with measured headroom, everything matched but the regularizer weight. Two things came out of it before any curve was long enough to read. All four arms reported an epoch-0 held-out reward of $0.541 \pm 0.071$ ($n{=}24$), identical to three decimals across four independent processes, which is the eval-determinism claim tested rather than asserted. And both regularizer terms demonstrably fire: \code{kl\_loss} $0.0009$ on the FlowGRPO reverse-KL arm, \code{data\_loss} $0.0000$ at $\beta{=}0$ against $0.2775$ at $\beta{=}0.3$, with the reported total reconciling exactly as $\text{rl} + \beta \cdot \text{data}$.

The curves themselves say nothing, and one accident showed why. The cluster killed one arm partway and it was resubmitted, producing two complete runs of the \emph{identical} configuration at the same seed:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\code{hb0} ($\beta{=}0$) & $n$ & peak & last 5 & mean after ep.\ 0 \\
\midrule
first run & 9 & 0.621 & 0.586 & 0.575 \\
rerun & 15 & 0.562 & 0.551 & 0.546 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The two differ in mean by $0.029$. The spread across the four different $\beta$ arms is also $0.029$. Run-to-run variation at a fixed configuration is the same size as the variation the sweep was built to detect, so depending on which run happened to survive, $\beta{=}0$ is either the best arm or tied for the worst. Every point of every arm lies inside one standard error of the untrained baseline.

Runs at one seed diverge because bf16 GEMM nondeterminism across processes is amplified by the RL feedback loop --- already known for a single epoch, now measured across a whole run. Since the eval latents are pinned and identical across runs, that divergence is the policy and not the measurement, so raising the eval sample count would not touch it. Replicate seeds are the only lever. Resolving a $0.03$ effect needs roughly four seeds per arm: $\beta \in \{0, 0.3\}$ at four seeds each, dropping the middle values on the grounds that if the endpoints do not separate, $0.01$ and $0.05$ will not.

The seeded comparison is now complete, and its analysis was written and committed before the runs finished, fixing in advance that the unit is a run rather than an eval point, that each run contributes its mean over a common epoch range, that the error comes from between-seed spread, and that a tag with several runs contributes its longest. At four seeds per arm:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
arm & mean & between-seed SD & $n$ \\
\midrule
\code{data\_beta}$=0$ & 0.5465 & 0.0180 & 4 \\
\code{data\_beta}$=0.3$ & 0.5607 & 0.0229 & 4 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Difference $-0.0143$ against a standard error of $0.0146$: $1.0$\,SE, no effect detected. The $95\%$ interval is roughly $[-0.044, +0.015]$, and the seed values interleave, $\beta{=}0$ spanning $0.5277$--$0.5688$ against $\beta{=}0.3$'s $0.5362$--$0.5897$.

The last run is worth dwelling on. At seven of eight runs the difference was $0.0225$ and projecting to about $1.7$\,SE; the eighth landed at $0.5362$, the lowest $\beta{=}0.3$ seed, and pulled it back to $0.0143$ --- the same value it had held at three-and-two seeds. Read in sequence the experiment said $0.9$\,SE, then $1.1$, then $1.5$, then $1.0$, and any of those quoted alone supports a different story. That is what the pre-registration was for, and it is also why an earlier draft of this section carried a projection that the remaining runs could not change the verdict: that projection was computed from a two-run-per-arm difference, which is the same error as reading a ranking off the first sweep. It has been withdrawn. A two-run standard deviation is not a standard deviation --- estimated from two runs it read $0.0276$, against $0.0229$ and $0.0180$ at four.

So the conclusion is a bound rather than a bare null. Anchor strength between $0$ and $0.3$ has no detectable effect on held-out counting accuracy over the first six epochs of this task, and any true effect is smaller than about $0.03$, which is the resolution four seeds per arm buys at a between-seed SD of $0.02$. Going further needs many more seeds, or a task where the anchor has more to do.

This is a negative result about the experiment as much as about $\beta$. It does not say the anchor is useless --- its job is to prevent drift over long training, and ten to fifteen epochs is early for drift --- only that this design cannot tell the arms apart, and that the previous design could not have either.

\section{Drift: the anchor over a longer horizon}

The comparison above ran to six epochs, which is early for the thing a data anchor is for. Its job is to keep the policy near the data manifold over long training, so a null at six epochs says little about it. Reaching twelve required fixing the reason nothing here ever ran that long: a segfault discarded all training and the run restarted from zero, so no experiment could outlast the mean time between crashes. With adapter, optimiser moments, epoch, advantage history and RNG state checkpointed every epoch, 73 crashes that previously produced about 70 curve resets became 5 crashes producing none.

The follow-up was pre-registered before the runs existed, with two statistics: \code{drift}, the late-third mean minus the early-third mean, and \code{giveback}, peak minus final. The second exists because testing the first against synthetic curves of known shape showed it returns exactly zero for a run that climbs and surrenders the whole gain --- which is the SFT give-back shape and precisely the failure the anchor should prevent. The registered statistic was blind to its own target.

Read once at the declared point, four seeds at $\beta{=}0$ against three at $\beta{=}0.3$, over a common twelve-epoch window:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
statistic & \code{data\_beta}$=0$ & \code{data\_beta}$=0.3$ & difference & \\
\midrule
drift & $+0.0007$ & $-0.0326$ & $+0.0334$, SE $0.0154$ & \textbf{2.2\,SE} \\
giveback & $+0.0676$ & $+0.0914$ & $-0.0238$, SE $0.0229$ & 1.0\,SE \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Both point the same way, and it is the opposite of the anchor's purpose: the unanchored arm is flat late in training while the anchored one declines and gives back more of its peak.

Four reasons this is not presented as established. Two statistics were registered, so under the null the chance of one exceeding $2$\,SE is $0.089$ rather than $0.045$. The anchored arm has three seeds, and a three-seed standard deviation is not a reliable one --- the same quantity read $0.0276$ from two runs against $0.0229$ from four. The statistic built for this shape, \code{giveback}, is the one that misses threshold. And it is one read, one task, one horizon. A replication on four fresh seeds per arm is running, pre-registered with its disconfirming outcome stated in advance: an opposite sign at any strength means this does not stand.

One disclosure. The analysis printed ``the anchor acts where it was meant to'' for a difference of either sign, so it would have described this result backwards. That sentence was corrected after seeing the result; no threshold, statistic or inclusion rule changed.

What this does establish is that the six-epoch null was not the whole story. Nothing was visible there and something is visible at twelve, which is an argument about horizon rather than about $\beta$.

\section{Still open}

The counting, CLIP-score, PickScore and HPS rewards all need re-measuring on fixed code, since their previous numbers came from rollouts that carried no signal. (An earlier version of this section said DiffusionDPO was still blocked on Pick-a-Pic gating, three lines after the paragraph explaining that it is not. It runs.) Two deliberate deviations are worth remembering: the log-prob is mean-reduced rather than summed, which is what makes \code{clip\_range}$=10^{-3}$ sane, and the counting reward is graded where GenEval's is binary, so those numbers are not directly comparable.
